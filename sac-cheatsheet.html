Side 1 · Analyse & Evaluate

The task, in one line

Analyse the organisation's development practices → identify and evaluate its security controls and threats → recommend improvements. Three verbs, three sections, five key skills.

The content barely changes between bands — the verb does. identifies → outlines → describes/discusses → explains/analyses/applies → analyses/justifies

The linkage moves that carry the top band

Almost every Very high descriptor asks you to connect back to something. If a section names none of these, it is capped below Very high however correct it is.

KS1→ the organisation's goals and objectives
KS2→ impact on the organisation (money, contracts, notification)
KS3→ criteria applied, measured, documented
KS4→ viable resolutions, named back to an Act or framework
KS5→ Med: legal obligations · High: goals & objectives · V.high: industry frameworks

Before submitting, highlight every goal, objective, Act and framework you have named.

KS1Analyse the current practices

Four things to cover: goals and objectives · in-house vs external development · security controls · threat modelling principles. If no measurable objectives are stated, say so and infer one — without a goal to link to, KS1 and KS5 are capped.

In-house vs external

In-houseExternal
Control of delivery, domain knowledge and client relationships stay inside; changes are cheap Buys specialist capability (incl. security) the organisation lacks; scales quickly
No specialist security capability; cost of maintaining the team; capacity limits Third-party exposure, IP and licence questions, less control, contractor practices unverified

The six security controls → what each closes

For each: does the organisation have it, how does it protect the practice and the data, and later — recommend it where missing.

ControlClosesE8 / ISM
Version control / repositoryIneffective code review; insider threats; lost codeBackups
Identity & access managementPoor IAM; insiders; credential reuseMFA; restrict admin
EncryptionMan-in-the-middle; exposure after a breach—

IAM has four parts — strong/multi-factor authentication · role-based access on least privilege · prompt deprovisioning · logging and review. MFA = two different kinds of evidence: something you know / have / are.

Encryption has two states — in transit (TLS) and at rest. Case studies often have one and not the other: "TLS on the portal but an unencrypted production database" is a specific, markable finding.

A control they have but do not enforce is worth more marks than one they simply lack — it lets you write about the gap between policy and practice.

Threat modelling — the three principles

Named in the Medium, High and Very high descriptors of KS1, and in every band of KS5 from Medium up. If your response never uses the phrase, you have capped yourself.

PrincipleAsk the case studyUsual finding
1. Define security requirementsWere they written down before building?Absent — issues fixed as customers report them
2. Identify & mitigate threatsSystematic, or reactive?Reactive — no risk register
3. Confirm mitigationIs anything audited, tested, reviewed after?Absent entirely

A control designed but never verified is not a mitigation, it is an assumption. Principle 3 is the one almost every organisation skips — saying so is free marks.

Because they perform none of the three principles, their controls were not chosen against identified threats — they accumulated by habit. That is why the controls they do have protect against risks they never faced, while the risk that materialised had no control at all.

KS2Vulnerability, threat, risk

The descriptors use all three and markers notice when they are treated as synonyms.

VulnerabilityA weakness in a system or practice
ThreatSomething or someone that could exploit it
RiskA threat exploiting a vulnerability, with a likelihood and an impact

A vulnerability is a fact. A risk is a judgement — and judgements are what get marked at the higher bands.

The ten vulnerabilities → what plants them

VulnerabilitySignal phrases in the case study
1. Use of APIs"integrates a third-party service", "cloud-based", "sends requests to" — and APP 8 if offshore
2. Malware"clicked a link", "downloaded an attachment", "systems were encrypted", a found USB
3. Unpatched software"two versions behind", "updates applied when there is time", "not restarted in months"
4. Poor identity & access management"the team shares a login", "no MFA", "still has access", "everyone has admin"
5. Man-in-the-middle"public Wi-Fi", "no VPN", "unencrypted connection", "the invoice details had changed"
6. Insider threats"a developer changed the configuration", "copied the data", "took files when they left"
7. Cyber security incidentsAnything already compromised — this is your evidence of impact for the top band. Use it.
8. Third-party software"a free library", "open-source component", "nobody checked the licence"
9. Ineffective code review"merged as soon as it compiles", "reviews when there is time", "no record is kept"
10. Combined dev/test/prod"all on the same server", "the test database contains live data", "work directly on the live site"

Insiders need not be malicious. A developer copying the live customer table into a test database to get realistic data is a careless insider threat — and in most case studies that is exactly what happened. If the insider risk is only potential, say it is latent rather than materialised.

Combined environments is usually the most serious finding, because it multiplies every other weakness: untested code reaches live, real customer data sits in dev, any dev machine reaches production.

Turning one vulnerability into a full-band answer

  1. Name it in study-design language → Very low / Low
  2. Explain the mechanism — how it compromises the practice or the data → High
  3. State the organisational impact — the money, the contract, the notification → Very high

Because the repository is protected by passwords alone, a single phished credential gives commit access to the codebase [mechanism] — which is how the March breach began, and it cost the Northern Logistics renewal [impact].

Go deep on three or four, not shallow on nine.

The lowest rating needs both minor impact and low probability. Rating lets you prioritise, and a prioritised list is what makes Section 3 read as analysis rather than a checklist.

KS3Criteria — propose, then apply

The Medium/High line is the word applies. Medium proposes criteria and says how they could be measured; High runs them against the case study and reaches a verdict; Very high adds accurate measurement and clear documentation.

Anatomy — all three parts, or you lose a band

  1. The question, phrased so it can be answered yes / partially / no
  2. The measure — what you would count, compare or inspect
  3. The data source — where that measurement comes from

Weak: "Is the code secure?" — unanswerable, so it cannot be applied.
Strong: "Is all code reviewed by a second developer before release?" · proportion of merges with a recorded reviewer · repository merge history.

A criteria set that covers the outcome

Pick five or six that match practices the case study actually describes.

Criterion (as a question)Measure · source
Are dev, test and production separated?Shared hardware? prod data in non-prod? · architecture docs
Is repository access restricted to current, authorised staff?Accounts vs staff register; MFA enrolment · audit logs
Is all code reviewed before release, and recorded?Merges with a named reviewer · merge history
Are OS and applications patched promptly?Mean days release → deployment · patch records
Is personal information encrypted in transit and at rest?TLS enforcement; DB encryption setting · config
Do developers complete secure-development training?Completion rate; phishing click rate · training records
Are security requirements defined and verified?Security requirements in the SRS; any audit · SRS, reports
Is data disposed of when no longer needed?Retention schedule; age of oldest records · policy

Document it as a table, one verdict per row

Use three verdicts, not two: met / partially met / not met. "Partially met" is the right answer whenever a practice exists but is not enforced, not recorded, or covers only part of what it should — which describes most real organisations.

Six criteria were applied and none was fully met: three failed outright, three partially. The failures cluster into environment separation and access management, and those two groups together account for the entire March incident chain. Current practices are therefore not effective at protecting either the development process or the data held in the product.

Efficiency = resources consumed (patching speed, time-to-detect). Effectiveness = how well it does its job. Most security criteria are effectiveness measures — saying which is which shows you know the difference.

KS4Legal and ethical

Most students write only the legal half, so the ethical half is where marks are easiest to gain.

LegalEthical
SourceAn Act of ParliamentProfessional & community expectations
TestDid they break the law?Was it the right thing to do?
ConsequenceInvestigation, penalty, enforceable undertakingReputational damage, loss of trust, censure

The strongest examples are both at once, because you can analyse them twice.

Privacy Act 1988 (Cwlth) — the named APPs

APP 1Open and transparent management — a clear, current privacy policy
APP 3Collect only what is reasonably necessary
APP 6Use or disclose only for the purpose collected (prod data used for testing; data pasted into an AI tool)
APP 8Reasonable steps before disclosing overseas (offshore API or cloud)
APP 9Do not adopt government-related identifiers as your own
APP 11Secure personal information, and destroy or de-identify it once no longer needed

APP 11 is the workhorse. "Reasonable steps" scales with the sensitivity of the data, the harm a breach would cause, and the organisation's size and resources — which is what makes it arguable, and arguable is what the discussion bands want.

For a company holding licence numbers, reasonable steps under APP 11 would clearly include encryption at rest and MFA — both inexpensive and standard. They had neither, so this is not a marginal judgement call.

Notifiable Data Breaches scheme

Breach likely to cause serious harm and not remediated → notify affected individuals and the OAIC as soon as practicable. Two reasons it is rich: the delay is a separate breach from the original failure, and it is simultaneously legal and ethical — both halves of KS4 in one example.

The other instruments

Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014 (Vic) — Victorian public sector and contracted service providers handling their data. If there is a state government client, this applies and most students miss it. IPPs 1, 2, 4, 5, 9 — IPP 4 (data security) is the counterpart of APP 11.

Copyright Act 1968 (Cwlth) — protects the expression of ideas, including source code; automatic, no registration. Appears as a library used outside its licence (open source is licensed, not obligation-free), code copied from a previous employer, or AI-generated code reproducing training material.

Legislation vs framework

LegislationBinding by law; penalties, investigation, enforceable undertakings
FrameworkNot law — but contractually binding the moment a client writes it in. Failure = lost contracts, failed audits

The four ethical issues

  • Ineffective security practices. Those exposed had no way to assess the risk and no realistic alternative — that asymmetry places a heightened obligation on the organisation, independent of the law.
  • Use of AI in development. Over-reliance — losing the ability to review, debug and maintain (this is the ethical issue, not AI use itself) · data disclosure to a third party · provenance · accountability — "the AI wrote it" is no answer to a regulator.
  • Intellectual property. Who owns the code, designs and data; open-source licence terms; IP settled in the contract when development is outsourced.
  • Copyright. Images, code, libraries or fonts used without a licence — "nobody would find out" is not a defence.

Two more that case studies plant

Disclosure and transparency — delaying or downplaying a breach to protect reputation. Usually the strongest available: the harm is concrete (people cannot freeze credit or watch for fraud) and it is also an NDB breach. Real customer data in development — APP 6 and APP 11 legally; ethically, they consented to a service, not to a test database.

Structuring an ethical answer — four moves

  1. Name the issue precisely
  2. Explain why it is ethically problematic — who is harmed, and how
  3. Apply it to the specific actions in the case study
  4. Consequence — legal (which Act, what penalty) and non-legal (reputation, trust, lost customers)

Resolutions must be viable — this organisation, with its stated resources, could actually do it. Name what changes, who does it, which obligation it satisfies. "Be more ethical" is not a resolution; "an incident-response plan naming a breach assessor, with a 72-hour escalation deadline" is.

KS5Recommend and justify

All three top bands want the same three ingredients — security controls, threat modelling principles, improvement strategies. What changes is what you tie them to:

Mediumrecommends + describes feasible methods → legal obligations
Highanalyses their impact → goals and objectives
Very highjustifies them → industry frameworks

Hit all three linkages and you have covered every band above Low.

The two named improvement strategies — both must appear

1. Onboarding / induction and ongoing developer training. Policies and why they exist; what data may be used in development; credentials; what may go to AI assistants; secure coding and review; phishing; refreshers, not one session on day one, measured by phishing simulation. Why it works: most incidents involve human behaviour, not a control failing — training addresses the cause, controls contain the symptom.

2. A documented risk management plan. Identifies each risk · rates likelihood and impact · assigns a mitigation · names an owner · sets a review cycle. Why it works: survives staff turnover, and creates the documentation an auditor asks for.

Mitigation menu — match it to what they actually have

Recommending a control for a problem the organisation does not have reads as a memorised list.

Sequence the recommendations, and say why

  1. Contain the biggest exposure — environment separation; production data out of development
  2. Close the entry point — identity and access management; most incidents begin with a credential
  3. Stop the recurrence — enforced code review, patching schedule
  4. Make it durable — threat modelling in the lifecycle, training, risk management plan
  5. Verify — audits, access reviews, re-run your Section 2 criteria

Step 5 is the one almost nobody writes — and it is explicitly the third threat modelling principle. Ending with how the organisation will confirm the mitigations worked closes the loop on your own evaluation.

The two frameworks

Essential Eight (ACSC): application control · patch applications · configure Office macro settings · user application hardening · restrict administrative privileges · patch operating systems · multi-factor authentication · regular backups. Maturity levels 0–3 say how completely each is implemented — contracts often specify "Maturity Level 2".

ISM (ACSC) — Guidelines for Software Development, three named: development, testing and production environments (separated; no production data in dev/test) · secure software design and development · application security testing. These map onto the most common SAC findings — usually the strongest framework to cite.

Sentence frames

KS1 → goal. This practice supports the objective of … because …

KS2 → impact. This vulnerability allowed … which resulted in … costing the organisation …

KS3 → verdict. Applying the criterion "…", the evidence shows … therefore this is partially met.

KS4 → both halves. Legally this breaches … under …; ethically, the harm falls on … who had no say in it.

KS5 → framework. Implementing … addresses …, satisfies … under …, and aligns them with … (Essential Eight / ISM), which the client's contract requires.

Before you hand it in

  • Goals and objectives named — and linked back to in KS1 and KS5
  • In-house vs external: advantages and disadvantages
  • All six controls: present or absent, protecting practices and data
  • Threat modelling named, all three principles, in KS1 and KS5
  • Vulnerabilities carry mechanism and organisational impact
  • Criteria applied in a table, a verdict per row, plus a synthesis
  • Legal and ethical, with viable specific resolutions
  • Recommendations sequenced: controls + threat modelling + strategies
  • KS5 references legal obligations, goals/objectives and a framework
  • Verification step — how they confirm it worked
